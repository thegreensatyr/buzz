What Rises After the Fall: Inside Satyr Arcana II

By DJ Green Satyr

Psychologist C.R. Snyder spent a career studying what he called "hope theory" — and one of his more counterintuitive findings was that hope is a trainable skill, made of two parts: believing you can find a path forward (what he called "pathways thinking"), and believing you have the will to walk it ("agency thinking"). People with high hope aren't people who never go through a Tower moment. They're people who've learned what to do the morning after one.

Satyr Arcana II, released Halloween 2026, is fifteen new cards built almost entirely out of what gets rebuilt after the fall. If the first deck was about carrying people forward through loss, the second is about what it actually takes to stand back up.

Card: Tower

Tower opens the second deck with no lyrics at all — just wordless, operatic vocalizations, because some collapses don't have language yet. That's accurate to how real crisis actually works: in the acute moment, most people aren't narrating. They're just enduring. The card doesn't rush you past that. It just tells you what comes next is also part of the deck.

Card: The Star

The Star is for the person who sang their biggest dream quiet to keep the peace at home, and kept hoping anyway — the exact definition of hope theory's "pathways thinking" under constraint. You don't need ideal conditions to hold onto hope. You just need to keep finding the next small path, even from inside a house where you've learned to stay quiet.

Card: The Sun

The Sun landed on Halloween 2026 on purpose — the one night everyone else chooses a mask, this card is for the person who never needed one. There's real psychological weight to that kind of consistency: people with a stable, authentic sense of self are measurably more resilient under stress, because they're not spending energy maintaining a performance on top of everything else they're carrying.

Card: The Fool

Most tarot decks treat the Fool as naive — card zero, a blank slate, someone who hasn't learned anything yet. The Fool rejects that completely: "he's already been through it, still walking like the night's not done." This is resilience research's actual finding about people who recover well from hardship — they're rarely the ones who haven't been tested. They're the ones who've been tested and kept walking anyway, eyes open, not because they don't know what it can cost, but because they do.

Card: The Builder

The Builder — also released in Spanish as El Corazón de un Constructor — is about trust that gets earned in increments, never handed over all at once. That's accurate relationship psychology. The strongest bonds are usually the ones that were tested a little at a time and held, not the ones that assumed trust from the start. Noticing someone's hands before you believe anything else about them is attention, not suspicion.

Card: Experience

Experience is for the person whose calm wasn't handed to them — it was earned by already surviving the thing you're currently panicking about. There's a reason people instinctively seek out someone like this during a crisis: their nervous system has already metabolized the event you're still inside of, and that steadiness is genuinely contagious.

Card: King of Cups — The One Who Stays

The One Who Stays is maybe the quietest card on the whole record, and the one that hits hardest for anyone who's watched people leave during the hard parts. It doesn't ask for applause. It's just a portrait of the rare, specific relief of someone who didn't go anywhere.

Card: King of Wands — He Brought the Music

Every rebuilding story needs someone who turns the lights back on. He Brought the Music is that card — rooted in rhythm, turntables, and a mic, for the person who understood that after a Tower moment, a grove needs someone willing to turn the volume back up on joy.

The whole arc, in order

Play the second deck front to back and you can hear hope theory happening in real time: the collapse (Tower), the quiet refusal to give up (Star), the return of an unguarded self (Sun), the decision to walk forward anyway (Fool), and the slow, deliberate work of building something sturdier than what fell (Builder, Experience, the court cards who carry it home). It plays like a working demonstration of resilience, in real time.

If you've survived your own Tower moment and you're still deciding what gets rebuilt, there's a card in this deck that already knows the shape of it.

Frequently asked questions

What is Satyr Arcana II?

Satyr Arcana II (also called Satyr Arcana — Part 2) is the second installment of DJ Green Satyr's 81-card musical tarot project, released Halloween 2026, adding fifteen new cards centered on rebuilding after loss.

What is hope theory?

Hope theory, developed by psychologist C.R. Snyder, treats hope as a trainable skill made of two parts: pathways thinking (believing a path forward exists) and agency thinking (believing you have the will to walk it).
