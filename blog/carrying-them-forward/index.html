Carrying Them Forward: The Psychology Behind the Satyr Arcana

By DJ Green Satyr

There's a well-documented idea in grief psychology called "continuing bonds." For decades, the dominant model of mourning said the healthy outcome was closure — you grieve, you process, you let go, you move on. Continuing bonds research, going back to the 1990s work of grief researchers Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman, and Steven Nickman, pushed back on that. What they found, over and over, in real people's actual lives: the people who adapt best to loss aren't the ones who let go. They're the ones who find a new, living relationship with the person who's gone — carrying them forward instead of leaving them behind.

This is the operating principle of the Satyr Arcana itself.

Satyr Arcana is a musical tarot deck — a full 81-card tarot, released across six albums, where every card is a song. Not an illustration inspired by someone. A song about someone, with card art that doubles as that single's cover. Some of those people are chosen family, lovers, collaborators who are very much alive. Some of them aren't. The deck doesn't sort them into separate piles. It treats a phone call with someone who's still here and a memory of someone who isn't as the same category of material: real relationships, worth building a myth around.

Card I: The Dream Traveler

The deck doesn't open with the Fool, like a standard tarot. It opens with The Dream Traveler — because this was never a blank-slate story. The card is a self-portrait: someone who visits the people he's lost in his dreams, and treats those visits as real conversation, not as something to "process" and set down.

Psychologically, this is continuing bonds in its most literal form. Dream-visitation is one of the most commonly reported — and most comforting — experiences among the bereaved, across cultures that otherwise have nothing in common. For a lot of people, the dream is the relationship, still open.

Card II: She Knew

She Knew is written for a best friend of 35 years, now gone, who held every secret without once using it against him. There's a specific kind of safety that comes from being fully known by someone — attachment researchers call it a "secure base," the relationship your nervous system calibrates against. Losing that person doesn't erase the calibration. The card is what it sounds like to keep carrying that security forward, instead of grieving it as something closed.

Card XIV: Temperance

This is the hardest card on the album, and the one with the most quietly radical psychology behind it. It's written for a woman who was institutionalized, abused, and nearly erased from memory — someone whose entire existence, by the end, was reduced to two whispered phrases: hello, and that she loved me. The artist is the last living person who remembers her.

There's a concept in trauma and memory work sometimes called "witnessing" — the idea that being truly seen, even once, even by one person, can matter more than almost anything else that happens to a person in crisis. Temperance is what it sounds like when someone decides that witnessing doesn't have an expiration date. The song doesn't ask you to feel sorry for her. It gives her back her balance.

Card XII: Perspective

Grief doesn't always show up as sadness. Sometimes it shows up as the people who stay through it. Perspective is about a man who'd been known for two days before he was asked to housesit — and then stayed, through a death, then another death, through flights and funerals and a leather competition and more loss, without ever once being asked to. They're married now. Post-traumatic growth research keeps finding the same pattern: people don't just survive catastrophic loss, they sometimes build the most important relationships of their lives inside it. This card is proof of work.

Card XIII: Santa Muerte

Written with and for the artist's collaborator and muse, the Goddess Persephone, who is very much alive. Santa Muerte is a high-energy, ceremonial tribute to Santa Muerte herself — Holy Death as guardian of transformation rather than an ending — with Persephone channeling her on the track.

Card: La Luna

La Luna is where She Knew and Santa Muerte resolve into each other. After writing Santa Muerte, the artist noticed something he hadn't gone looking for: Persephone greeting him with the same terms of endearment Natasha used, giving the same kind of advice, connecting on the same frequency. La Luna is the realization of that pattern, and the decision to trust it — "I can't see it but I feel it." A new person echoing a lost one's exact register of care, continuing her rather than replacing her, is continuing bonds finding a new door to come through.

Why music carries grief better than words

The Satyr Arcana moves across genres the same way grief does — dance tracks, folk songs, rock, spoken word, all sitting in the same deck because no single genre can hold everything a life contains. That range isn't an accident. Music entrains the nervous system in a way plain talk doesn't; shared listening and singing together are among the oldest, most cross-cultural ways humans have processed loss. Wakes have music. Funerals have hymns. The Satyr Arcana takes that instinct — in whatever form a given story calls for — and builds an entire mythology out of it.

Frequently asked questions

What is Satyr Arcana?

Satyr Arcana is a musical tarot deck by DJ Green Satyr: a full 81-card tarot released across six albums, where every card is a song and the single's cover art doubles as that card's tarot art.

What is "continuing bonds" in grief psychology?

Continuing bonds is a model of grief, developed from the 1990s research of Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman, and Steven Nickman, holding that healthy adaptation to loss comes not from closure but from maintaining a new, living relationship with the person who died.

Is Satyr Arcana a dance album?

No. Satyr Arcana spans multiple genres across its released decks — electronic/dance, folk, rock, R&B, industrial, and spoken word — with the genre of each song following the story it tells.

Who is Satyr Arcana written about?

Each card is written about a real person in the artist's life, living or deceased — chosen family, lovers, collaborators, and relatives — treated as one continuous category of material rather than split into separate "memorial" and "tribute" songs.

How can someone find their own card?

A free, 15-question reading at GreenSatyr.Buzz matches a person's own experience to one of the cards across both released decks.
